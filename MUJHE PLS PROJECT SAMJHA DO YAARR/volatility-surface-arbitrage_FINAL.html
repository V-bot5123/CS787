01 / 05

The ground truth baseline

Carr–Madan (1999) FFT

Before a system can call anything mispriced, it needs an honest reference for what correct looks like. That reference is built once, mathematically, with no learning involved.

Raw NIFTY inputs — spot, strike, time to expiry, the risk-free rate — feed a Fourier-transform pricer that integrates over the characteristic function of the return distribution. The payoff is speed: instead of pricing one option at a time, the FFT produces an entire grid of theoretical prices in one pass, which is what makes a full, continuous volatility surface computationally realistic to hold in memory.

That FFT price grid is inverted to a model implied-volatility surface (per-expiry Variance Gamma calibration, then Newton inversion on Black–Scholes) before anything is compared — the gap between that model IV and the messy, real, quoted market IV is where every anomaly this system will ever find actually lives.

02 / 05

Detection & temporal weighting

CNN · LSTM · Attention · DNN

Subtract the model IV surface (Phase 1) from the real, quoted market IV and what's left is a residual matrix — computed with a leave-out fit, and filtered against the bid-ask spread so illiquid noise isn't mistaken for a signal. We call this a mispricing signal, not an arbitrage — it is relative value against our own model, not a riskless profit. Four components take turns interrogating that map.

A CNN runs convolutional filters across it to read spatial gradients — the shape of the disagreement. An LSTM carries that shape forward across ten trading days, holding a memory of how it's evolving. An attention layer then decides which of those ten days actually matter, assigning each a weight. A final DNN collapses everything into a per-cell score map: a mispricing signal with a confidence score attached. Labels for training come from synthetic bump injection and a reversion criterion (never from the residual threshold itself), and a rolling z-score is kept as the baseline this stack has to beat.

03 / 05

Dimensionality reduction

Variational Autoencoder

A full volatility surface is hundreds of data points wide. Before it can condition a generative model, it has to become small enough to actually hand off — while keeping the information that matters.

A VAE encoder maps the raw surface into a probabilistic latent distribution — a mean μ and variance σ² for each latent dimension — and is trained to minimize KL divergence (with KL warm-up, to avoid the latent collapsing and carrying no information). What comes out the other side is a tiny vector, three to eight numbers, that this pipeline calls the market-state latent. We deliberately keep it small: the base paper's own PCA already explains ~90% of the surface's variance with 3 components, so the VAE only earns its place if it beats a PCA baseline of the same size on downstream survival and hedging metrics.

04 / 05

Generative stress testing

Arbitrage-Penalized VolGAN Extension

An anomaly is only worth trading if it survives what the future might actually do. Our generator takes random noise and the compressed market-state latent, and generates thousands of futures for the joint pair (return, log-IV increment) — matching the base paper, which VolGAN's authors found necessary to reproduce the observed negative link between spot moves and volatility moves.

We train two versions and compare them, rather than assuming the fancier one wins. The paper-faithful baseline uses binary cross-entropy plus Sobolev smoothness penalties (Lm, Lτ) on moneyness and maturity, weighted by gradient-norm matching — this is what keeps VolGAN's surfaces smooth, and arbitrage is corrected afterward by re-weighting scenarios, not built into the loss. Our extension adds an explicit arbitrage penalty Π = p₁ + p₂ + p₃ (calendar, monotonicity, and butterfly violations, not just calendar) directly into the loss, and separately ablates a Wasserstein-GP objective against the BCE baseline. Neither surface is guaranteed arbitrage-free — we report the penalty, we don't assume it's zero.

05 / 05

Explainability & evaluation

LangGraph · multi-agent LLM

Everything upstream produces tensors and probabilities. Nothing upstream produces a sentence a human can act on. That's the job of the final stage.

A deterministic rule makes the call first: each of the 10,000 scenarios reprices and hedges the flagged trade, and a scenario "survives" if its hedged PnL clears a stated loss limit — the EXECUTE/ABORT decision is a hard-coded threshold on that survival ratio and CVaR, nothing a language model chooses. A multi-agent LLM then ingests the anomaly coordinates from Phase 2 and the survival statistics from Phase 4 as structured JSON, and explains — in plain English, with every number it quotes checked against that JSON — the decision the rule already made.
